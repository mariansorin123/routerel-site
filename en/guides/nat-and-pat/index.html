NAT and PAT Explained: Static, Dynamic and Overload (2026)

The Routerel team· Updated: November 9, 2026· 15 min read

Nearly every network you'll configure uses NAT. The idea is simple, but the four address terms (inside local, inside global, outside local, outside global) and the order of the commands trip up a lot of students, and that's exactly where exam questions land.

This guide covers why NAT exists, what each term means, how static NAT, dynamic NAT and PAT work, how to configure them step by step in Cisco IOS, how to see what the router is actually doing, and how to publish an internal server with port forwarding. At the end there's a troubleshooting table and exercises with answers.

In the Networking Academy CCNA v8 courses, NAT is in CCNA 3 · CEN, module 8 (NAT for IPv4).

Why NAT exists

An IPv4 address is 32 bits long, which gives about 4.3 billion addresses in total. That isn't enough for today's Internet. IANA handed out its last large free blocks in 2011, and RIPE NCC, the European registry, made its final /22 allocation in November 2019.

The emergency fix that became standard practice has two parts:

  1. Private addresses (RFC 1918). Three ranges set aside for internal networks. Anyone can use them, as many times as they like, because they're never routed on the Internet.
  2. NAT. At the network edge, the router swaps the private address for a public one. Replies come back to the public address, and the router hands them to the right device.

swipe

Private range Prefix Addresses
10.0.0.0 – 10.255.255.255 10.0.0.0/8 16,777,216
172.16.0.0 – 172.31.255.255 172.16.0.0/12 1,048,576
192.168.0.0 – 192.168.255.255 192.168.0.0/16 65,536

A classic exam trap: 172.32.5.1 is not private. The /12 range stops at 172.31.255.255. If you're unsure where a range ends, check it with the subnet calculator.

There's also 100.64.0.0/10 (RFC 6598), which ISPs use for CGNAT, meaning NAT done on their side, before your router. If your home router gets a WAN address from that range, your traffic is going through two layers of NAT.

NAT has downsides too, and they show up in theory questions:

  • it breaks end-to-end connectivity: an outside device can't open a connection to an inside one unless you add a static rule;
  • it makes troubleshooting and tracing harder: in an external server's logs, the whole company shows up as one address;
  • some protocols struggle: those that carry IP addresses inside their payload need help from the router, and IPsec needs NAT-T (UDP 4500) to get ESP through NAT; IPsec with AH, which protects the IP header itself, doesn't work through NAT at all;
  • it adds a little delay, because the router rewrites every packet and recalculates checksums.

The lab topology

Every example uses the same small network. The public addresses come from the ranges reserved for documentation (192.0.2.0/24, 198.51.100.0/24, 203.0.113.0/24), so they don't belong to anyone real.

  • R1 has 198.51.100.2/30 facing the ISP and a default route: ip route 0.0.0.0 0.0.0.0 198.51.100.1.
  • The ISP has also assigned the public block 203.0.113.0/29 to the company and routes it to R1. Those addresses aren't configured on any interface; R1 only uses them in translations.
  • WEB is some server out on the Internet.

The four terms: inside/outside, local/global

Each term has two halves, and each half answers a different question:

  • inside / outside = whose device it is: on your network (inside) or beyond it (outside);
  • local / global = where you're looking from: from inside your network (local) or from the outside (global).

swipe

Term What it is In our example
Inside local an inside host's address, seen from the inside 192.168.10.11 (PC1)
Inside global the same inside host's address, seen from the outside 203.0.113.1
Outside local an outside host's address, seen from the inside 192.0.2.80 (WEB)
Outside global the outside host's real address, seen from the outside 192.0.2.80 (WEB)

Walkthrough: PC1 opens a page on WEB

  1. PC1 sends a packet with source 192.168.10.11 and destination 192.0.2.80. To PC1, both addresses are "local": that's how it sees them.
  2. The packet enters R1 on G0/0/1 (inside) and leaves on G0/0/0 (outside). R1 replaces the source with 203.0.113.1 and records the mapping in its NAT table.
  3. On the Internet, the packet travels with source 203.0.113.1 (inside global) and destination 192.0.2.80 (outside global).
  4. WEB replies to 203.0.113.1. The ISP routes that block to R1.
  5. R1 finds the mapping, rewrites the destination back to 192.168.10.11 and forwards the packet to PC1.

Why are outside local and outside global the same? Because R1 only translates inside addresses. They'd differ only if you also translated outside addresses (with ip nat outside source), for example when two companies using the same private ranges connect to each other. In almost every CCNA scenario they're equal.

A memory hook: "global" is always the address that travels on the Internet, and "local" is the one you see on the LAN.

The three kinds of NAT

swipe

Type How it maps addresses When you use it
Static NAT one inside address ↔ one fixed public address, permanently servers that must be reachable from the Internet
Dynamic NAT inside addresses ↔ free addresses from a pool, one to one, while in use rarely today; when you have plenty of public addresses
PAT (NAT overload) many inside addresses ↔ one public address (or a few), told apart by port almost everywhere: homes, offices

Static NAT

You write the mapping by hand and it sits in the table permanently, even when no traffic flows. It works in both directions: anyone on the Internet can open a connection to 203.0.113.5, and R1 passes it to the server. That's why it's used for public-facing servers (web, mail, VPN).

Dynamic NAT

You define a pool of public addresses and an ACL that says which inside addresses are allowed to be translated. The first host to send traffic gets the first free address in the pool. The mapping expires after a period with no traffic (24 hours by default for simple translations without ports) and the address goes back into the pool.

The catch: the pool has a fixed size. With 4 public addresses, only 4 inside hosts can be out at once. The fifth gets no translation, and its packets are dropped until an address frees up.

PAT (NAT overload)

PAT translates the source address and the source port. Each connection gets a unique "public address : port" pair, so thousands of connections can share a single public address. When a reply comes back, R1 looks at the destination port and knows exactly whose it is.

Ports are 16 bits, so in theory one public address can carry tens of thousands of simultaneous connections per protocol (TCP, UDP). Cisco IOS tries to keep the original source port; if another connection is already using it, the router picks a different free port.

ICMP (for example ping) has no ports, so the router uses the ICMP query identifier instead.

Configuration step by step

Whatever the type, the steps are the same:

  1. mark the LAN-facing interface with ip nat inside;
  2. mark the ISP-facing interface with ip nat outside;
  3. define what to translate (an address for static, an ACL for dynamic and PAT);
  4. define what to translate it into (an address, a pool or the interface address);
  5. tie it together with an ip nat inside source ... command.

Steps 1 and 2 are always the same:

R1(config)# interface GigabitEthernet0/0/1
R1(config-if)# ip nat inside
R1(config-if)# exit
R1(config)# interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0
R1(config-if)# ip nat outside
R1(config-if)# exit

If you have several inside networks (say, subinterfaces for several VLANs), each one needs ip nat inside. Forget one subinterface and a whole VLAN loses Internet access.

Static NAT: an inside server gets a fixed public address

R1(config)# ip nat inside source static 192.168.10.50 203.0.113.5

Order matters: inside address first (inside local), then the public one (inside global). From now on, anyone reaching 203.0.113.5 from the Internet lands on SRV.

Dynamic NAT with a pool

R1(config)# access-list 1 permit 192.168.10.0 0.0.0.255
R1(config)# ip nat pool PUBLIC 203.0.113.1 203.0.113.4 netmask 255.255.255.248
R1(config)# ip nat inside source list 1 pool PUBLIC
  • The ACL is a standard ACL that permits the inside addresses to translate. It takes a wildcard mask, not a subnet mask: 0.0.0.255 for a /24. For an awkward prefix like /22, subtract the mask from 255.255.255.255 (the subnetting guide has the details).
  • The pool has a name, a first and last address, and the block's mask (or prefix-length 29 instead of netmask). I left .5 out of the pool because the static NAT already uses it. A pool that overlaps a static translation causes conflicts.
  • The ACL doesn't filter anything here. A deny just means "don't translate", not "block".

PAT with a pool

Add one word to the command above:

R1(config)# ip nat inside source list 1 pool PUBLIC overload

The router uses the pool addresses and shares each one, by port, across many connections.

PAT on the interface address (the one you'll see most)

When the ISP gives you a single public address, the one on your WAN interface, you don't need a pool at all:

R1(config)# access-list 1 permit 192.168.10.0 0.0.0.255
R1(config)# ip nat inside source list 1 interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0 overload

Every device on the LAN goes out as 198.51.100.2. That's exactly what your home router does. A nice side effect: if the WAN interface gets its address by DHCP and the address changes, PAT follows it automatically.

R1's full configuration

This is the version used in the rest of the guide: one server published with static NAT, everything else through PAT on the interface.

R1(config)# interface GigabitEthernet0/0/1
R1(config-if)# ip address 192.168.10.1 255.255.255.0
R1(config-if)# ip nat inside
R1(config-if)# no shutdown
R1(config-if)# exit
R1(config)# interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0
R1(config-if)# ip address 198.51.100.2 255.255.255.252
R1(config-if)# ip nat outside
R1(config-if)# no shutdown
R1(config-if)# exit
R1(config)# ip route 0.0.0.0 0.0.0.0 198.51.100.1
R1(config)# access-list 1 permit 192.168.10.0 0.0.0.255
R1(config)# ip nat inside source list 1 interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0 overload
R1(config)# ip nat inside source static 192.168.10.50 203.0.113.5

When a packet could match both a static and a dynamic translation, the static one wins. So SRV always goes out as 203.0.113.5, even though its address also matches the ACL.

Verification

swipe

Command What it shows
show ip nat translations the NAT table: every active translation with all four addresses
show ip nat translations verbose the same table, plus each entry's age and time left before it expires
show ip nat statistics inside/outside interfaces, active translation count, hits/misses, pools
clear ip nat translation * removes all dynamic translations (static ones stay)
debug ip nat prints every translated packet in real time

show ip nat translations

After PC1 and PC2 both open the page on WEB, and PC2 also runs a ping:

R1# show ip nat translations
Pro Inside global         Inside local          Outside local         Outside global
--- 203.0.113.5           192.168.10.50         ---                   ---
tcp 198.51.100.2:51544    192.168.10.11:51544   192.0.2.80:443        192.0.2.80:443
tcp 198.51.100.2:1024     192.168.10.12:51544   192.0.2.80:443        192.0.2.80:443
icmp 198.51.100.2:7       192.168.10.12:7       192.0.2.80:7          192.0.2.80:7

Reading it line by line:

  • The first line is the static translation. It has no protocol and no outside addresses, because it isn't tied to any particular connection.
  • PC1 went out with its original port, 51544.
  • PC2 happened to pick the same source port. 198.51.100.2:51544 was already taken, so R1 gave it port 1024. That's how the router keeps the replies apart.
  • The icmp line shows the ICMP query ID (7) where a port would be.

[Id: 1] access-list 1 interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0 refcount 3

What to look at when troubleshooting:

  • are the inside and outside interfaces the right ones? This is where a swapped configuration jumps out at you;
  • "extended" means translations with ports, i.e. PAT;
  • "refcount" is how many active translations are using that rule;
  • with a pool, you'll also see a line like total addresses 4, allocated 4 (100%), misses 12: the pool is full and 12 attempts didn't get an address.

The layout varies slightly between IOS versions.

clear ip nat translation

R1# clear ip nat translation *

This flushes the dynamic translations; active connections drop and rebuild on the next packet. Use it before changing the NAT configuration: IOS may refuse to change or remove a pool that's still in use.

debug ip nat (handle with care)

R1# debug ip nat
IP NAT debugging is on
NAT: s=192.168.10.11->198.51.100.2, d=192.0.2.80 [40211]
NAT*: s=192.0.2.80, d=198.51.100.2->192.168.10.11 [0]

s is the source, d the destination, and the arrow shows which address was rewritten. The asterisk in NAT* means the packet was translated in the fast path (CEF). The number in brackets is the IP packet's identification field.

A warning: on a production router, debug ip nat prints a line for every translated packet and can load the CPU until your session freezes. In production, limit it with an ACL (debug ip nat 10, where ACL 10 permits only the host you're chasing) and turn it off as soon as you're done with undebug all. Over SSH you also need terminal monitor to see the messages.

Port forwarding with static PAT

A different scenario: a smaller company has a single public address (198.51.100.2, the interface address), no 203.0.113.0/29 block, and wants its internal web server reachable from the Internet. A regular static NAT would bind the whole public address to the server, leaving nothing for the rest of the LAN. The answer is static PAT: you map just one port.

R1(config)# ip nat inside source static tcp 192.168.10.50 443 interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0 443

Or with the address written out:

R1(config)# ip nat inside source static tcp 192.168.10.50 443 198.51.100.2 443

Now a connection from the Internet to 198.51.100.2:443 reaches 192.168.10.50:443, and PAT for the rest of the LAN keeps working.

The ports don't have to match. For an internal SSH server you want to expose on an unusual public port:

R1(config)# ip nat inside source static tcp 192.168.10.60 22 198.51.100.2 2222

Connections to 198.51.100.2:2222 reach port 22 on 192.168.10.60. In the NAT table, these rules show up as permanent tcp entries with no outside addresses.

Two practical points:

  • the inside server needs R1 as its default gateway, or its replies won't come back the same way;
  • port forwarding exposes the service to the whole Internet. Only forward what genuinely has to be public, and protect it (ACLs, a firewall, strong authentication).

Common mistakes

swipe

Symptom Likely cause How to check and fix
NAT table is empty, nothing reaches the Internet ip nat inside and ip nat outside swapped show ip nat statistics (interface list), then fix each interface
Some hosts get out, others don't the ACL doesn't cover every inside network, or the wildcard is wrong show access-lists (match counters), fix the wildcard or add a line
The ACL never matches a subnet mask was typed instead of a wildcard (255.255.255.0 instead of 0.0.0.255) show running-config | include access-list
The first few PCs work, the rest don't dynamic NAT without overload, pool exhausted show ip nat statistics (pool 100% allocated, misses) → add overload
A whole VLAN has no Internet its subinterface is missing ip nat inside show running-config interface G0/0/1.20
Translations appear but replies never arrive the ISP isn't routing the pool addresses to you, or the default route is missing show ip route, confirm the assigned block with the ISP
A published server doesn't answer from outside wrong protocol or port in the static PAT, or no gateway on the server show ip nat translations, the server's settings
You can't change the pool it's in use by active translations clear ip nat translation *, then change it

Two rules prevent most of these: inside faces the LAN, outside faces the ISP, and the NAT ACL permits the inside addresses, with a wildcard mask.

One detail that matters once you start writing filtering ACLs: for a packet going from inside to outside, the router routes first and translates second; for a packet coming in from outside, it translates first and routes second. Inbound ACLs are checked before either step, so an inbound ACL on the outside interface sees the public (inside global) addresses, not the private ones.

NAT and IPv6

IPv6 addresses are 128 bits, enough for every device to have a globally unique address (GUA). The main reason for NAT, running out of addresses, goes away. A typical IPv6 network gets a /64 prefix on each segment, and devices talk to each other directly, end to end.

"But doesn't NAT protect me?" Not in the way that counts. The protection comes from a stateful firewall that only lets in replies to connections started from the inside. Routers and firewalls do that just as well without rewriting addresses.

What you'll still come across in IPv6:

  • NAT64: lets an IPv6-only client reach an IPv4-only server. It's a transition tool, not a replacement for IPv4 NAT;
  • NPTv6 (RFC 6296): translates one IPv6 prefix into another, without ports. It's rarely used.

To learn IPv6 addressing from scratch, see the IPv6 addresses guide and the IPv6 tool, which shortens and expands addresses.

Exercises

  1. The NAT table shows tcp 203.0.113.3:1027 10.1.1.20:50112 192.0.2.10:80 192.0.2.10:80. Which is the inside local, inside global and outside global address? What type of NAT is this?
  2. You have dynamic NAT with a pool of 3 public addresses, no overload, and 20 PCs. What happens when a fourth PC tries to open a web page while the first three are still active?
  3. The inside networks are 172.16.0.0/24, 172.16.1.0/24, 172.16.2.0/24 and 172.16.3.0/24. Write the ACL (a single line) and the PAT command for interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0.
  4. After configuring NAT, show ip nat statistics shows "Outside interfaces: GigabitEthernet0/0/1" and "Inside interfaces: GigabitEthernet0/0/0", and G0/0/0 connects to the ISP. What's wrong and how do you fix it?
  5. You have one public address, on G0/0/0, and an internal HTTPS server at 192.168.10.70. Write the command that publishes it on port 443 without breaking PAT for the rest of the LAN.
Answers

1. Inside local: 10.1.1.20. Inside global: 203.0.113.3. Outside global (and outside local): 192.0.2.10. The public port (1027) differs from the inside port (50112), so the router translated the port too: this is PAT.

2. The fourth PC gets no translation, and its packets are dropped. Dynamic NAT without overload gives each host a whole address. It will only work once one of the translations expires or is cleared. The fix: overload.

3. Together the four networks make up 172.16.0.0/22, and the wildcard for a /22 is 0.0.3.255:

R1(config)# access-list 1 permit 172.16.0.0 0.0.3.255
R1(config)# ip nat inside source list 1 interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0 overload

Plus ip nat inside on the LAN-facing interface (or interfaces) and ip nat outside on G0/0/0.

4. The roles are reversed. The fix:

R1(config)# interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0
R1(config-if)# no ip nat inside
R1(config-if)# ip nat outside
R1(config-if)# exit
R1(config)# interface GigabitEthernet0/0/1
R1(config-if)# no ip nat outside
R1(config-if)# ip nat inside

Then clear ip nat translation * to get rid of any bad translations.

5.

R1(config)# ip nat inside source static tcp 192.168.10.70 443 interface GigabitEthernet0/0/0 443

This is static PAT: it binds only TCP port 443, so every other port is still free for the LAN's dynamic PAT.

The fastest way to learn NAT

  • Build this topology in a simulator and try every variant, including dynamic NAT with a tiny pool so you can watch it run out.
  • After each variant, read show ip nat translations and say out loud which address is inside local and which is inside global.
  • Break it on purpose: swap inside and outside, type a subnet mask instead of a wildcard, drop overload. Then find the fault using only show commands.
  • Practise wildcard masks with the binary converter and the subnet calculator; you'll need them again for ACLs.
  • Brush up on the basics with the Cisco IOS commands cheat sheet.

Frequently asked questions

What is NAT?

A router function that rewrites the source or destination IP address of a packet as it crosses between the inside and outside networks. Most often, private LAN addresses are translated into a public address so devices can reach the Internet.

What is the difference between NAT and PAT?

Plain NAT (static or dynamic) maps one inside address to one public address. PAT, also called NAT overload, translates the port as well, so many inside devices can share the same public address at the same time. The router tells them apart by port number.

What do inside local and inside global mean?

Inside local is the address of a device on your network as seen from the inside, usually a private address. Inside global is the address of that same device as the outside world sees it, which is the public address it was translated to.

What does the overload keyword do?

It turns on PAT: the router translates ports as well as addresses. Without overload, dynamic NAT hands out a whole public address per inside device, and once the pool runs out, the next devices can't get out.

Why is show ip nat translations empty?

The usual causes: ip nat inside and ip nat outside on the wrong interfaces, an ACL that doesn't match the inside addresses, a missing route to the Internet, or simply no traffic yet. Dynamic translations only appear after an inside device sends its first packet.

Do I need NAT with IPv6?

Normally, no. IPv6 has enough addresses for every device to get a globally unique address, so there's no need to hide a whole network behind one address. Protection comes from a stateful firewall, not from address translation. NAT64 exists, but it's a transition technique between IPv6 and IPv4. For where NAT fits in the exam, see our guide to CCNA.
