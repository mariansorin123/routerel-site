CCNA Jobs in 2026: Entry-Level Roles and What Employers Want

The Routerel team· Updated: November 9, 2026· 10 min read

A CCNA isn't a job offer. It's proof that you understand how data moves through a network and that you can configure and troubleshoot the basic gear. To a hiring manager, that means you won't be starting from zero the first time a switch port misbehaves.

This article covers the entry-level roles where a CCNA genuinely helps, what you'd actually do in each, what employers expect on top of the certification, how to land a first job without experience, and where to go next. Salary figures are indicative and sourced at the end.

Entry-level roles where the CCNA counts

swipe

Role What you do day to day Who hires
NOC technician monitor the network, open and escalate incidents ISPs, telcos, managed service providers, data centers
Service desk L2 handle the tickets L1 can't close, many of them network-related IT outsourcing firms, large enterprises, multilingual service hubs
Junior network administrator / engineer configure switches, routers and Wi-Fi under a senior's guidance systems integrators, banks, retail, manufacturing, public sector
Field engineer / field technician install and replace equipment on site integrators, carriers, equipment vendors
Sysadmin with networking keep all the IT of a small or mid-sized business running SMEs, schools, clinics, local government
Junior SOC analyst (sometimes) triage security alerts security firms, SOC providers, banks

NOC technician

A NOC (Network Operations Center) watches the network around the clock. You work in front of a monitoring system that flags links going down, interfaces logging errors and devices that stop responding. When an alarm fires, you check quickly (ping, traceroute, show commands), open a ticket, notify the customer or carrier, and escalate if it's beyond you.

Shifts are common, including nights and weekends, and many NOC roles serve customers across several countries. The upside: in one year you'll see more real incidents than in five years of labs.

Service desk L2

L1 answers the phone and fixes the simple things: passwords, accounts, applications. What's left goes to L2, and a lot of it is networking: a laptop that won't get a DHCP lease, a switch port in the wrong VLAN, a VPN client that won't connect, Wi-Fi that keeps dropping in one meeting room.

This is where the CCNA pays off immediately. Someone who understands DHCP, VLANs and NAT solves in ten minutes what someone else escalates.

Junior network administrator or engineer

The job most people picture when they start the CCNA. You configure ports and VLANs, rack and patch new switches, back up configurations, upgrade firmware in maintenance windows and document everything. At first you work under a senior engineer, and significant changes go through a change management process.

Plenty of job ads for this role ask for one or two years of experience. Don't let that stop you: solid projects plus a year in a NOC or on a service desk often fill that gap.

Field engineer

You go to the customer: mount equipment in racks, run and test cabling, install wireless access points, confirm everything works and swap out failed hardware. The configuration often comes prepared by a central team, but you need to know how to apply it and verify the result. Ads often ask for a driving licence and a willingness to travel.

Sysadmin with networking

In a company of 30 to 150 people, one or two people look after everything: Windows servers, accounts, email, backups, the firewall, switches and Wi-Fi. The CCNA covers the network side, but the role demands just as much Windows Server and Microsoft 365. It's a great place to learn a lot, fast.

Junior SOC analyst

A SOC (Security Operations Center) receives alerts from security tools and triages them: false alarm or real attack? The CCNA helps because alerts are full of addresses, ports, protocols and traffic flows. Most employers still want a security certification alongside it, so here the CCNA is a plus rather than the ticket in.

What employers want beyond the CCNA

Read 20 job ads for the roles above and the same requirements keep coming back:

  • Linux basics: finding your way around a shell, permissions, editing config files, ip, ss, journalctl. Many network devices and almost every monitoring server run Linux.
  • Windows Server and Active Directory: users and groups, Group Policy, DNS and DHCP on Windows. Essential for service desk and sysadmin roles.
  • Firewalls: rules, NAT, site-to-site and remote-access VPNs on gear from different vendors. The logic is the CCNA's; only the interface changes.
  • English, and often another language: in international teams, tickets, documentation and meetings are in English. A second language such as German, French, Dutch or Italian opens many service desk roles across Europe.
  • Basic scripting and automation: Python (say, a script that backs up every switch config), PowerShell on Windows, a first look at Ansible. Nobody expects you to be a developer, just to automate something repetitive.
  • Cloud basics: what a virtual network (VPC/VNet) is, subnets, route tables and security rules in AWS or Azure. They're CCNA concepts behind a different console.
  • Soft skills: explaining a problem to a non-technical person, writing a ticket the next shift can follow, staying calm when the network is down.

You don't need all of them on day one. Two or three, plus a CCNA, already put you ahead of most applicants.

What it pays: indicative figures

There's no single reliable salary figure for "CCNA jobs" across Europe. Pay depends on the country, the city, languages, shift allowances and the type of employer, and the gap between, say, Western and Eastern Europe is large. The most useful reference is the pay posted in real job ads for your role and your city.

For an official benchmark, the US Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes data on network and computer systems administrators:

What it measures Figure Source and period
Median annual wage, network and computer systems administrators (US, all experience levels) $99,130 BLS, May 2025
Lowest-paid 10% of the same occupation earned less than $62,640 BLS, May 2025
Projected employment change for the same occupation (US) −4% BLS, 2025–2035

Two things to take from this:

  • a median across all experience levels isn't a starting salary. Entry-level NOC and service desk roles typically pay well below the figure for experienced administrators;
  • the classic "admin" job is shrinking in the US projection. BLS points to routine tasks being automated, some work moving to DevOps-focused developers and some being outsourced to network-as-a-service providers. That's exactly why the scripting and cloud skills above matter.

On the European side, demand is real but uneven. Eurostat counted 10.45 million ICT specialists in the EU in 2025, 5.0% of everyone employed, with shares ranging from 8.9% in Sweden to 2.5% in Greece.

How to get your first job without experience

Eurostat found that more than half of the EU businesses that tried to hire ICT specialists struggled to fill those roles, and candidates' lack of experience was one of the reasons they gave. So your job is to show experience before you have a job. It's doable.

1. Build a home lab

  • Packet Tracer (free with a Networking Academy account) covers everything on the CCNA.
  • GNS3 or EVE-NG run real device images and Linux machines, closer to production.
  • Second-hand gear: an old switch and router bought cheaply teach you things a simulator won't (the console cable, a port that won't come up, password recovery).

2. Document projects, not just exercises

A recruiter can't see what you did in Packet Tracer unless you show them. Put three or four projects on GitHub, each with a topology diagram, the configurations and a short README: what you set out to do, what you chose and why, what broke and how you fixed it.

Ideas that look good:

  • a fictional office network with three VLANs, DHCP, PAT to the Internet and ACLs between departments (with an addressing plan built using subnetting);
  • OSPF between three routers, with a backup link and a documented failover test;
  • a Python script that connects to devices over SSH and saves their configurations;
  • a monitoring server on an old Linux box that watches your home router.

3. Look for internships and placements

Large telcos, vendors and IT service firms run internship and graduate programmes, often recruiting months in advance. University placements, volunteering for a charity or a school, and a real project for a small business all count too: it's a real network with real users.

4. Use the service desk as a way in

A year on a service desk or in a NOC gives you exactly what your CV is missing: real incidents, tickets, procedures and colleagues who can vouch for you. In larger organizations, moving internally from support to the network team is a common path. Say in the interview that this is where you're heading.

5. A CV that gets past the first filter

  • one page, in English and, if you're applying locally, in the local language;
  • with no experience, put "Projects" above "Certifications", with a link to your GitHub;
  • be concrete: "Designed and configured a three-VLAN network with router-on-a-stick and PAT" says far more than "networking knowledge";
  • mirror the keywords from the ad that you genuinely know (VLAN, OSPF, Active Directory). Many companies screen CVs automatically.

In the interview, expect practical questions: a subnetting problem on paper, "what happens when you ping a website?", or some show output to interpret. For that last one, the Cisco IOS commands cheat sheet helps.

What comes after the CCNA

Pick the direction based on the job you want, not on a list of certifications.

swipe

Direction Next step For which roles
Networking CCNP Enterprise: the 350-401 ENCOR core exam plus one concentration exam of your choice network engineer, network architect in the long run
Security CCNA Cybersecurity (formerly CyberOps Associate), CCNP Security, vendor firewall certifications SOC, network security engineer
Cloud an entry-level AWS or Azure certification, then a cloud networking one cloud engineer, infrastructure engineer
Automation CCNA Automation (formerly DevNet Associate), Python, Ansible network automation engineer

Since February 2026, Cisco has moved its DevNet (automation) and CyberOps (cybersecurity) certifications under the CCNA and CCNP names (CCNA Automation and CCNA Cybersecurity, for example). The certification most people mean by "CCNA" is still the networking one, exam 200-301. The exam format, cost and validity are covered in our guide to CCNA.

One practical tip: after the CCNA, work for six to twelve months before starting the CCNP. What you learn on the job gives the professional-level material its meaning.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get a job with just a CCNA and no experience?

Yes, usually an entry-level one: NOC technician, service desk L2, field technician or junior network administrator. The CCNA gets your CV past the first filter, but in the interview the hands-on projects you can show, and how well you explain them, count just as much.

Which job is easiest to get with a CCNA?

NOC and service desk roles have the most openings for beginners. They're also the best springboards: you see real incidents, learn how a large organization works and can move toward network administration from there.

How much does a network administrator earn?

It depends heavily on the country, city and experience. As a reference point, the US Bureau of Labor Statistics put the median annual wage for network and computer systems administrators at $99,130 in May 2025, across all experience levels. European salaries are generally lower and vary a lot between countries, so check job ads in your own market.

What do I need to know besides the CCNA?

Employers often ask for Linux basics, Windows Server and Active Directory, firewalls, good English, a bit of scripting (Python or PowerShell) and cloud basics. Communication, documentation and working with a ticketing system matter just as much.

Is the CCNA still worth it in 2026?

Yes, if you want to work in networking, infrastructure or security. CCNA concepts such as addressing, routing, switching, NAT and ACLs underpin cloud and security work. The certification doesn't guarantee a job, but it gives you a foundation employers recognize.

What certification comes after the CCNA?

It depends on your direction. For networking, CCNP Enterprise (a core exam plus a concentration exam). For security, CCNA Cybersecurity or CCNP Security plus vendor firewall certifications. For cloud, an entry-level AWS or Azure certification, then a cloud networking one. For automation, CCNA Automation.
