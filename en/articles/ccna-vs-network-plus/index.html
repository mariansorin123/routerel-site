CCNA vs CompTIA Network+: Which One to Choose in 2026

The Routerel team· Updated: November 9, 2026· 9 min read

Look for a first networking certification and two names come up straight away: Cisco's CCNA and CompTIA's Network+. Both are entry level, both last three years and both cost a few hundred dollars. Beyond that, they're less alike than they look.

Below we compare them using details checked on the official Cisco and CompTIA pages (on November 9, 2026), then give you a straight answer on which one we'd pick in which situation. The links are in the Sources section at the end.

The core difference: one vendor or none

CCNA (Cisco Certified Network Associate) is vendor-specific. The concepts are universal (IPv4 and IPv6 addressing, subnetting, VLANs, Spanning Tree, OSPF, NAT, ACLs, wireless, security), but you configure and troubleshoot them in Cisco IOS. The official exam topics are full of verbs like "configure" and "troubleshoot": you set up OSPF, NAT/PAT and ACLs, and you diagnose problems with show commands, ping and traceroute.

Network+ is vendor-neutral. It won't ask you for any particular vendor's commands. In exchange, it's broader: cabling and physical installations, cloud and virtualization concepts, network operations, security, and a structured troubleshooting method along with the right tool for each job.

In practice: after CCNA, you can build a Cisco switch and router configuration from scratch. After Network+, you can talk about any network accurately and pick the right fix in a scenario, but you won't necessarily have practiced configuring a specific box.

That's a difference in purpose, not quality. What you learn for CCNA carries over to other vendors anyway; only the command syntax changes.

A few details worth knowing:

  • Network+ pricing. The certification page doesn't show a fixed price, and the CompTIA store shows the price for your region. In December 2024, CompTIA's official blog gave a price of USD 369, but it has gone up since: according to a CompTIA training partner, the list price has been USD 399 since May 29, 2026. Check the CompTIA store before you buy a voucher. CompTIA charges for every attempt; there are no free retakes.
  • CCNA pricing. Cisco's exam page lists USD 300. You'll see the final price, with tax, in your Pearson VUE account when you book.
  • No official CCNA numbers. You'll find plenty of question counts and passing scores quoted online, but Cisco publishes neither. Don't plan around them.

Versions and timing

Both exams are close to a refresh, so your booking date matters.

CCNA: the last day for version 1.1 is February 2, 2027, and version 2.0 goes live on February 3, 2027. The code stays 200-301 and the length stays 120 minutes. The new version has five domains, one of them dedicated to AI and network operations. Our CCNA guide has the details.

Network+: the current version, N10-009 (V9), launched on June 20, 2024. CompTIA says exams usually retire about three years after launch and estimates N10-009's retirement in 2027, without an exact date on the certification page.

The names tell the story. Network+ puts nearly a quarter of the exam on troubleshooting as a method and a toolkit. CCNA puts half on infrastructure and switching, with real configuration work (VLANs, trunks, EtherChannel, Rapid PVST+).

Difficulty and hands-on work

This is our view, not an official figure: for most candidates, CCNA is harder.

That's not because Network+ is easy. Network+ asks about a lot of things, just less deeply. CCNA covers fewer topics but expects you to know them down to the command and the show output. You need to read a routing table and say where a packet goes next, work out why an OSPF adjacency won't form, or predict which port Spanning Tree will block.

Both demand fast, reliable subnetting. If it still trips you up, start with the subnetting step-by-step guide and practice with the subnet calculator.

The hands-on side differs too:

  • for CCNA, passing without labs is hard. You need a simulator (Cisco Packet Tracer is free with a Networking Academy account) or real gear, and plenty of hours at the CLI;
  • for Network+, labs help but aren't tied to a vendor. It matters more that you understand the concepts, recognize symptoms and know which tool fits the problem, such as a cable tester or a protocol analyzer.

Job market recognition

There's no official data comparing the two across Europe, so we'll stick to what we can actually say.

  • CCNA is the better-known name in networking. Cisco gear is everywhere in enterprises and service providers, and Cisco Networking Academy courses are taught in many universities and schools across Europe. Almost any IT recruiter knows what a CCNA is.
  • Network+ is very well known in the US, partly because CompTIA maps it to work roles in the NICE and DoD 8140 frameworks, which matter for US government jobs and contractors. In Europe you'll see it more often at companies with US clients or in IT support roles.
  • Neither one guarantees a job. For a first role, what you can show in an interview counts just as much: explaining how a packet gets from a PC to the internet, subnetting on the spot, walking through a lab you built yourself.

The best test is one you run yourself. Search a job site or LinkedIn for the role you want in your city ("network engineer", "NOC analyst", "IT support") and note which certifications the ads mention. Ten real job ads will tell you more than any article.

Where each one leads

Each certification sits on a different ladder.

  • Cisco: after CCNA comes CCNP (for example CCNP Enterprise), then CCIE. It's the natural path if you want to stay in networking.
  • CompTIA: Network+ usually sits between A+ (tech support) and Security+ (security). It's a good route if security or general IT operations appeal to you.

Which one should you pick?

Pick CCNA if:

  • you want a networking job (network administrator, network engineer, NOC);
  • you're already taking CCNA courses at a university, school or Cisco academy;
  • you enjoy configuring things, not just understanding them;
  • you're aiming for CCNP later.

Pick Network+ if:

  • you're new to IT and want broad foundations rather than deep ones;
  • you're heading for tech support, systems administration or security, and networking is only part of the job;
  • you work, or want to work, for an employer that explicitly asks for Network+, for example on US projects;
  • you plan to follow the CompTIA path to Security+.

Should you get both? Usually not. The material overlaps a lot, and the second exam costs another few hundred dollars and a few more months. The exception is when a specific employer wants Network+ and you already hold CCNA, or the other way round. If you're starting from zero, can afford one networking exam, and networking is the goal, CCNA gives you more for your money.

One last practical point: if you choose CCNA and start now, at a typical pace of 3–6 months, you'll most likely sit version 2.0. Study from the new exam topics.

Frequently asked questions

Which is harder, CCNA or Network+?

For most people, CCNA. Network+ covers a wide range of topics at a conceptual level, while CCNA goes deeper and expects you to configure and troubleshoot devices in Cisco IOS (VLANs, OSPF, ACLs, NAT).

Can I take CCNA without Network+?

Yes. Cisco has no prerequisites for CCNA, and Network+ is not a required step. Many people go straight to CCNA.

How long are CCNA and Network+ valid?

Both for three years. You renew CCNA by retaking the exam, passing a higher-level Cisco exam or earning 30 CE credits. You renew Network+ with 30 CEUs plus the CE fee (USD 150 per three-year cycle), or without the CE fee by passing the latest Network+ exam, a higher-level CompTIA certification or the CertMaster CE course.

What languages are the exams available in?

CCNA in English and Japanese. Network+ in English, German, Japanese, Portuguese and Spanish.

Which one do employers value more?

It depends on the market and the role. CCNA is the better-known name for networking jobs, especially where Cisco equipment is common. Network+ carries a lot of weight in the US, including for roles mapped to the DoD 8140 framework. Check real job ads for the role you want.

Is it worth getting both?

Rarely. The content overlaps heavily, and each exam costs a few hundred dollars. It makes sense only if a specific employer asks for Network+, or if you plan to follow the CompTIA path toward Security+.
