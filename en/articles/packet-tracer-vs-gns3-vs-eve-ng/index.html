Packet Tracer vs GNS3 vs EVE-NG: Which One for Your CCNA Labs (2026)

The Routerel team· Updated: November 9, 2026· 10 min read

Everyone tells you CCNA is learned hands-on. Fewer people tell you what to put your hands on. Packet Tracer, GNS3 and EVE-NG come up in every thread, and picking the wrong one costs you either money or weeks spent fighting installers instead of learning networks.

This is a practical comparison: what each tool does, what it costs, what hardware it needs, where Cisco images legally come from, and what we'd pick at each stage of your studies. Prices, editions and requirements were checked against the official pages on November 9, 2026; the links are at the end.

Simulation vs. emulation

This one distinction explains most of what follows.

A simulator is a program that imitates what a router or switch does. Cisco built Packet Tracer from scratch: when you type show ip route, you aren't running Cisco IOS, you're running a model of it. The upside is that everything is light and starts instantly. The downside is that the model is incomplete. Some commands are missing, some options don't exist, and some outputs are simplified.

An emulator runs the device's real operating system inside a virtual machine or container. In GNS3 or EVE-NG, a router boots a virtual version of the same software you'd find on production gear. The commands, error messages and outputs are genuine. The cost: you need images, a capable computer and some patience with setup.

Think of it like aviation training. A simulator is a flight sim on your desk; an emulator is a real cockpit bolted to the hangar floor.

Cisco Packet Tracer

Packet Tracer is the official lab tool for the Cisco Networking Academy courses, and it's free. You download it from the Networking Academy Resource Hub, or through the free "Getting Started with Cisco Packet Tracer" course, once you've created an account. The first time it starts, you sign in with your NetAcad account, and you can choose to stay signed in for three months. It runs on Windows, Linux and macOS; there's no phone or tablet version.

What it does well:

  • Instant start. Drag a router, a switch and two PCs onto the canvas, cable them up and start configuring.
  • Simulation mode. You can step through every packet: the ARP request, the reply, the ICMP echo crossing the router. For understanding how traffic actually flows, nothing beats it.
  • Auto-graded activities. Course labs ship as .pka files that check your configuration for you.
  • A wide device range. Routers, Layer 2 and Layer 3 switches, wireless access points and controllers, servers running DNS, HTTP or DHCP, even IoT devices.

Where it stops:

  • it implements only part of the Cisco IOS command set, so now and then a command from the documentation is missing or an option isn't there;
  • you can't run other network software, other vendors' images or real Linux machines;
  • it doesn't realistically model performance or the subtle failures you get on real networks.

For CCNA, those limits barely matter. The courses were built around it, so almost every exam topic has a working Packet Tracer lab.

GNS3

GNS3 is a free, open-source emulator released under the GNU General Public License version 3. It comes in two parts: the graphical client you install on your computer, and a server that actually runs the devices. The server can run locally, but the recommended setup is the GNS3 VM, a Linux virtual machine that works with VirtualBox, VMware Workstation, VMware ESXi, KVM/QEMU or Hyper-V.

What it does well:

  • it runs real software: Cisco images (if you have them legally), routers and firewalls from other vendors, Linux machines and Docker containers;
  • it connects to your real network, so you can mix virtual devices with a physical switch or the internet;
  • you can capture traffic with Wireshark directly on any link in the topology;
  • it has a marketplace of templates ("appliances") that set up a device type for you, once you import the image yourself.

Where it gets in the way:

  • installation takes several steps: the app, the VM, virtualization enabled in the BIOS, the images;
  • every device eats real memory, so bigger topologies need a serious machine;
  • it ships with no Cisco images at all (more on that below).

EVE-NG

EVE-NG (Emulated Virtual Environment, Next Generation) is also an emulator, but it's designed as a server platform: you install it as a virtual machine or on bare metal and work from a web browser. Several people can share one server, which is why it's popular with companies and training centers.

Something important changed in 2026: the Community edition reached end of support and end of life in June 2026. Its last release is still on the download page, marked as such. The current product is EVE-NG Professional (7.4.0-2, released November 1, 2026), which downloads in Freemium mode:

Seven nodes sounds tight, but most CCNA labs (three routers and three switches, say) fit inside it.

Before you choose it, know this:

  • the official documentation supports VMware Workstation Pro 16 or later, VMware ESXi 6.7 or later, Ubuntu Server 24.04 on bare metal, and Google Cloud;
  • VirtualBox, Hyper-V and Apple M-series Macs are not officially supported;
  • the full install package is around 10 GB.

Cisco images: the part many tutorials skip

Packet Tracer is the only one of the three that ships with "Cisco devices" built in. GNS3 and EVE-NG are just platforms. To run a Cisco router on them you need a Cisco image, and that image must be legitimately licensed for your use.

The essentials:

  • GNS3's official documentation says that, for legal reasons, it cannot provide Cisco images: you have to supply your own.
  • Images floating around forums or shared archives "ready to download" are not a legitimate source. Beyond the legal problem, you have no idea what's inside them.
  • Legitimate routes lead back to Cisco one way or another. The GNS3 documentation mentions downloading from Cisco under a service contract, Cisco lab products that include virtual images, and images from physical devices you own.
  • Whatever the route, read the image's license and make sure it allows the platform you plan to use. Cisco states, for example, that using Cisco Modeling Labs images outside CML without a proper license is prohibited, so you can't move them into GNS3 or EVE-NG.

A fourth option: Cisco Modeling Labs Free

If you want real IOS without the licensing headache, look at CML-Free, the free tier of Cisco's own virtual lab platform. It's single-user, runs up to 5 nodes at a time (unmanaged switches and external connectors don't count) and includes IOL, IOL-L2 and ASAv images. That's enough for a CCNA lab with two or three routers and two switches, and the images are legal from day one, inside CML.

For Packet Tracer, we couldn't find official requirements for the current release on the pages we were able to check. In practice it runs on any laptop you'd use for school or office work.

With GNS3 and EVE-NG, memory is what holds you back. Every virtual router or switch takes its own share of RAM, and heavier images (IOS XE routers, for example) need several GB each. IOL images are by far the lightest.

How far each tool takes you for CCNA

Packet Tracer covers essentially the whole CCNA 1–3 syllabus: addressing and subnetting, basic IOS setup and SSH, VLANs and trunks, Spanning Tree, EtherChannel, static routing and OSPF, DHCP, HSRP, ACLs, NAT and wireless basics. It's also what the course labs are built in, so the course material and the lab tool fit together perfectly.

GNS3 and EVE-NG add genuine output. That matters for exam questions that show you a show command from real equipment and ask what's broken. They also let you break things without a simulator's limits. They aren't required for CCNA, but they prepare you better for the actual job.

Wireless is the exception: it's hard to reproduce in an emulator, so Packet Tracer (or real hardware) remains the easiest way to practice it.

Our recommendation by stage

Starting CCNA 1 · ITN. Packet Tracer only. Don't burn time on virtual machines and images. Use Simulation mode to see what packets really do, and redo the course labs until you can build them without instructions.

In CCNA 2 · IEN or CCNA 3 · CEN. Packet Tracer stays your main tool. Every now and then, rebuild a lab on real IOS: CML-Free is the simplest legal way to do that. If you already have licensed images and a machine with 16–32 GB of RAM, GNS3 works just as well.

Looking beyond CCNA (CCNP, security, automation, multi-vendor). This is where emulators become essential. EVE-NG Professional on a server or a powerful workstation is the comfortable choice for big labs with browser access. GNS3 is the free, more flexible alternative, at the cost of more setup work. Budget for legal images too; they often cost more than the platform.

Getting started

  1. Create your Networking Academy account and install Packet Tracer, even if you plan to move to an emulator later.
  2. Enable virtualization in the BIOS if you want GNS3 or EVE-NG. Without it, virtual machines run painfully slowly or not at all.
  3. Start small. Two routers and two switches teach you more than a 20-node network you don't understand.
  4. Save your configs to a text file after every lab. You can paste them back any time and compare what changed.
  5. Break something on purpose (a wrong mask, a shut interface) and fix it using only show commands.

A first lab in 20 minutes

In Packet Tracer, place an ISR4321 router, two 2960 switches and two PCs:

PC0 --- S1 --- G0/0/0 [R1] G0/0/1 --- S2 --- PC1
192.168.1.10   192.168.1.1   192.168.2.1     192.168.2.10
  1. On R1: hostname R1, address 192.168.1.1/24 on G0/0/0 and 192.168.2.1/24 on G0/0/1, then no shutdown on both.
  2. On the PCs: the address and default gateway (192.168.1.1 for PC0, 192.168.2.1 for PC1).
  3. Ping PC1 from PC0. The first ping may lose a packet while ARP does its work.
  4. Switch to Simulation mode, filter for ARP and ICMP only, and step through the path.
  5. Bonus: add an ACL on R1 that blocks pings from PC0 to PC1 but allows everything else. If you get stuck, the steps are in our Cisco ACLs guide.

Keep the Cisco IOS commands cheat sheet open for the basics. Once you're comfortable, rebuild the same lab in an emulator and compare the show ip interface brief and show ip route output.

Frequently asked questions

Is Packet Tracer enough for the CCNA?

For the vast majority of topics, yes. It covers basic device setup, VLANs, Spanning Tree, OSPF, ACLs, NAT and DHCP. Some commands are missing and some outputs are simplified, so it helps to see real IOS output along the way, but it isn't required.

Is Packet Tracer free?

Yes. You download it from Cisco Networking Academy after creating a free account, and you sign in with that account when you first launch it. It runs on Windows, Linux and macOS, but not on phones or tablets.

What is the difference between simulation and emulation?

A simulator such as Packet Tracer imitates how devices behave using purpose-built software. An emulator such as GNS3 or EVE-NG runs the device's real operating system inside a virtual machine, so the commands and outputs are the real ones.

Do GNS3 and EVE-NG come with Cisco IOS images?

No. Both are platforms only. You supply the Cisco images yourself, and you need the legal right to use them. GNS3 states plainly that it can't provide Cisco images for legal reasons.

Is there still a free version of EVE-NG?

The Community edition reached end of support in June 2026. The Professional edition now downloads in Freemium mode, which is free and limited to 7 nodes per instance. A Pro Base license costs 150 EUR per year.

What kind of computer do I need for GNS3 or EVE-NG?

A CPU with hardware virtualization enabled in the BIOS, and plenty of RAM. GNS3 recommends 16 GB on Windows. For EVE-NG Professional, the documentation asks for at least 16 GB of RAM for the virtual machine alone, so a 32 GB machine is far more comfortable in practice.
