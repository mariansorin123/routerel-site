CCNA v2.0 (2027): What Changes and What to Do If You're Studying Now

The Routerel team· Updated: November 9, 2026· 10 min read

On February 3, 2027, the CCNA 200-301 exam moves to version 2.0. If you're studying right now, that leaves you with a real decision: race to take the current version (v1.1), or prepare for the new one from the start?

The basics (cost, format, a 12-week study plan) are in our guide to CCNA. This article goes further. We compare the two official topic lists item by item, look at what's new, what's gone and what the AI domain actually asks for, and then get to the decision and a study plan. Every fact is checked against the Cisco documents listed at the end.

The official timeline

swipe

When What happens
May 2026 Cisco announces the new CCNA (CCNA 2.0)
July 1, 2026 Cisco publishes its official advice: take v1.1 now, don't wait
February 2, 2027 last day to sit v1.1
February 3, 2027 v2.0 becomes the only version available

There's no overlap period. From February 3 you can no longer choose.

What stays the same: the 200-301 code, the 120-minute length, and the outcome: passing earns you the CCNA certification, the same one as before. If you get your CCNA on v1.1, it stays valid until its expiration date even after the exam is retired.

What isn't officially confirmed (as of November 9, 2026): the number of questions, the passing score and the exact item types for v2.0. Cisco doesn't publish those for v1.1 either. The exam page still lists a price of USD 300 and English and Japanese as languages, without a separate note for v2.0. A Cisco blog post mentions "practical assessments" and "more troubleshooting tasks" in v2.0, with no detail on format. If you see precise claims online about the new exam's format, check where they come from before you rely on them.

The domains: v1.1 vs v2.0

1.0 Network Fundamentals 20% 1.0 Network Infrastructure and Connectivity 25%
2.0 Network Access 20% 2.0 Switching and Network Access 25%
3.0 IP Connectivity 25% 3.0 IP Routing 20%
4.0 IP Services 10% 4.0 Network Services and Security 20%
5.0 Security Fundamentals 15% 5.0 AI, and Network Operations and Management 10%
6.0 Automation and Programmability 10%

The domain names changed, but much of the content simply moved. Here's where the major topics land:

Security no longer has its own domain. It sits with network services in domain 4.0 (20%), and Cisco says it's woven through the whole exam. Routing drops from 25% to 20%, while switching rises from 20% to 25%. The list is also much shorter: v1.1 has 53 top-level objectives, v2.0 has 29 (many with sub-items).

What's new in the official list

Comparing the two documents line by line, v2.0 explicitly names topics that weren't in v1.1:

  • OSPFv3 for IPv6. The OSPF objective becomes "single area OSPFv2 for IPv4 and OSPFv3 for IPv6", with neighbor authentication explicitly excluded. To shore up the basics, see our OSPF guide.
  • More Layer 2 security: alongside DHCP snooping, DAI and port security, you now get storm control and RA guard (protection against rogue IPv6 Router Advertisements).
  • DNS by record type: diagnosing problems with A, AAAA, CNAME, MX, NS and PTR records.
  • SFTP and SCP for moving configurations and IOS images, instead of the old TFTP/FTP objective.
  • Hands-on AAA: local usernames, plus configuring the device as a TACACS+ or RADIUS client. In v1.1 you only had to compare and describe.
  • NAT/PAT on IOS XE routers, with PAT named explicitly.
  • Troubleshooting with real tools: show commands (including logs), extended ping, traceroute and reading packet capture output.
  • Clients: instead of just verifying IP settings on Windows, macOS and Linux as in v1.1, you troubleshoot wired and wireless connectivity, including wireless security settings.
  • Rapid PVST+ moves from "interpret" to "configure".
  • Edge ports: switch port attributes (VLAN, PoE, port channel, LACP) for desktops, printers, IoT devices, access points, IP phones, virtualized hosts and network appliances.
  • CDP and LLDP with a new purpose: using them to validate that network documentation matches reality.
  • Infrastructure as code, named explicitly as a management approach.
  • Agentic AI and prompts (more below).

What's no longer named

The v2.0 list drops topics that v1.1 called out by name:

  • NTP (client/server configuration);
  • QoS (classification, marking, queuing, policing, shaping);
  • TFTP/FTP (replaced by SFTP/SCP);
  • TCP vs UDP as a standalone objective;
  • network components and topology architectures (two-tier, three-tier, spine-leaf, SOHO);
  • switching concepts as a standalone objective (MAC learning, flooding, the MAC address table);
  • Cisco wireless architectures and AP modes, plus WLC GUI configuration and WPA2-PSK in the GUI;
  • REST APIs and JSON, Terraform, and SDN architecture (overlay, underlay, fabric, northbound/southbound APIs);
  • security program elements and password policies;
  • BPDU filter (BPDU guard, root guard and loop guard stay);
  • VRFs from the virtualization objective.

Two caveats matter here. First, the official document says that related topics not on the list may still appear. Second, a lot of this knowledge is still needed indirectly. You can't troubleshoot DHCP without knowing it runs over UDP, or a switching problem without understanding the MAC table. In practice: stop studying these as separate topics, but don't throw them out.

The verbs matter: more troubleshooting

The verbs at the start of each objective tell you what kind of question to expect, so they're worth counting.

swipe

Verb v1.1 v2.0
Troubleshoot 0 objectives 6 objectives
Diagnose 0 2
Configure 16 (nearly all "configure and verify") 8
Describe / Explain / Compare 28 6

IPv4 and IPv6 addressing, client connectivity, DHCP and static routing are all phrased as troubleshooting in v2.0. That lines up with Cisco's own description on its blog: more troubleshooting tasks and a different approach to depth.

You'll see forum posts claiming that "troubleshooting is 30% of the exam". That figure isn't in any official Cisco document; the only official weights are the per-domain ones in the table above.

The new domain: AI and network operations

Domain 5.0 carries 10% and combines AI with network management (SNMP, syslog, Ansible, and device-based, cloud-based, controller-based, automation-based and infrastructure-as-code approaches). AI isn't starting from zero: v1.1 already had an objective on generative and predictive AI. The difference is that v2.0 makes it practical.

The objectives ask for three things:

  1. Describe the role of agentic AI in network operations. A regular assistant answers a question. An agentic system takes a goal ("find out why branch X can't reach the server"), plans its own steps, runs tools (show commands on devices, for example), interprets the results, and proposes or applies a fix. Whoever operates the network needs to know what such an agent can do and where a human has to sign off.
  2. Select a suitable prompt for a generative AI system, considering four components Cisco names explicitly: data classification, output format, persona and instructions.
  3. Evaluate output and recommendations from AI assistants during troubleshooting. This one is written directly into the exam description.

Here's an example we wrote to show the difference between a weak prompt and a good one:

Weak prompt:
  OSPF is broken, what do I do?

Better prompt:
  You are a network engineer troubleshooting single-area OSPFv2 on Cisco
  IOS XE routers. Below is the output of "show ip ospf neighbor" and
  "show ip ospf interface" from two adjacent routers. I have replaced public
  addresses and passwords with X. Tell me the most likely reason the
  neighbors are stuck in EXSTART and which command would confirm it.
  Answer in 5 bullet points or fewer, and don't suggest config changes
  before verification.

The second prompt has a persona (network engineer), the data the model needs, care with data classification (no passwords or public addresses), the output format you want, and clear instructions.

Evaluating the recommendation is where your networking knowledge comes in. If the assistant replied "change the hello interval", you should know that's wrong: neighbors stuck in EXSTART almost always point to an MTU mismatch, while mismatched timers stop the adjacency much earlier. AI doesn't replace the fundamentals; it makes them matter more.

What hasn't been published: what the AI questions look like on the exam (what interface, what kind of task). Don't build your prep around unofficial descriptions.

Take v1.1 by February 2, or wait for v2.0?

Cisco's official advice is clear: take v1.1 now, because what you learn carries over into v2.0, and waiting only delays your certification. That's sound advice, but it depends on where you're starting. From today (November 9, 2026) to February 2, 2027 is roughly 12 weeks.

swipe

Your situation Our suggestion
You've been studying for months and consistently score above 80–85% on practice exams Book v1.1 for December or the first half of January
You've covered about half the material Take a readiness check around mid-December. If you're close, go for v1.1; if not, switch to v2.0 with no regrets
You're starting from scratch now Aim for v2.0 in spring 2027. Twelve weeks from zero, alongside a job or classes, is a big risk of rushing
You've already failed v1.1 once Rebook v1.1 quickly if your mistakes were few and concentrated in a handful of topics

Why mid-January rather than February 1: if you fail, you have to wait 5 calendar days, counted from the day after the exam, before you can retake it. Seats in the last days before the retirement may also be harder to find. If your first attempt is by mid-January, you still have room for a second one before February 2. Cisco also sells bundles that include two attempts (Exam Safeguard).

If you miss the deadline, you don't lose what you've learned. Switch to the v2.0 list, add the new topics and drop the ones that are gone.

How to plan your studies

Step 1: the shared core, whichever version you take

These topics appear in both lists and make up most of the exam. Start here:

  • IPv4 addressing and subnetting, IPv6 addressing (see the subnetting guide, the IPv6 guide and the subnet calculator);
  • VLANs, trunks, inter-VLAN routing, EtherChannel with LACP, Rapid PVST+ (see the VLANs and trunks guide);
  • the routing table, static routes, OSPFv2 (see the OSPF guide), HSRP and VRRP;
  • DHCPv4, NAT, standard and extended ACLs, DHCP snooping, DAI, port security, IPsec VPNs at the "describe" level;
  • SNMP, syslog, Ansible, wireless principles, CDP and LLDP.

Step 2a: if you're going for v1.1

Add the v1.1-only topics: NTP, QoS, TFTP/FTP, REST APIs and JSON, SDN architecture, Cisco wireless architectures and WLC GUI configuration, network components and topologies. Do at least two timed practice exams before your booked date.

Step 2b: if you're going for v2.0

Add the new topics: OSPFv3, storm control, RA guard, DNS record types, SFTP/SCP, AAA with TACACS+ and RADIUS, NAT/PAT on IOS XE, reading packet captures, client troubleshooting on Windows, macOS and Linux, agentic AI and prompts. Shift your focus from "I know the definition" to "I can find the fault": in the lab, break one thing at a time on purpose and fix it using only show commands, ping and traceroute.

Study materials

Check which version every book or course covers. Material written for v1.1 is still useful for the shared core, but it won't cover the new topics. Cisco says its new official training on Cisco U. prepares you for both versions. If you're learning through Networking Academy, ask your instructor which version of the courses you're on; for the exam itself, the official topic list is the source of truth.

Frequently asked questions

When does CCNA v2.0 start?

On February 3, 2027. The last day to sit v1.1 is February 2, 2027. The two versions don't overlap.

Is the CCNA exam code changing?

No. Both versions use the code 200-301 and last 120 minutes. What changes is the topic list: v2.0 has five domains instead of six, one of them covering AI and network operations.

Is a CCNA earned on v1.1 worth less than one earned on v2.0?

No. Both exams lead to the same CCNA certification, valid for three years. When Cisco retires an exam, certifications earned with it stay valid until their own expiration dates.

What AI knowledge does CCNA v2.0 require?

According to the official exam topics: describe the role of agentic AI in network operations, select a suitable prompt for a generative AI system (data classification, output format, persona, instructions), and evaluate output and recommendations from AI assistants during troubleshooting. The domain that contains AI is weighted at 10%.

Should I wait for CCNA v2.0 or take v1.1 now?

If you can be ready by mid-January 2027, take v1.1; that's also Cisco's official advice. If you're starting from scratch now, it's more realistic to aim for v2.0 and study from the new list, beginning with the shared core.

What is removed from CCNA in v2.0?

The official list no longer names, among other things, NTP, QoS, TFTP/FTP, REST APIs and JSON, WLC GUI configuration and SDN architecture. Cisco notes that related topics can still appear, so the underlying theory remains useful.
